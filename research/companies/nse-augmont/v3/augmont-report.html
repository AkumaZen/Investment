What the company does

Augmont runs two platforms on one engine. SPOT is an online bullion counter for about 5,300 jewellers, dealers and manufacturers. It brought in 93% of FY26 revenue. Gold For All sells coins, digital gold, EMI jewellery and gold-loan services to consumers through apps and partners. It brought in 7%, but is growing fastest. Behind both sit two refineries with 284 tonnes a year of capacity, the second-largest in India.

Why revenue is big but profit is small

In FY26 Augmont sold metal worth Rs 94,186 cr and made Rs 348 cr of profit. That is about 37 paise for every Rs 100 of sales. So revenue growth alone says little. What matters is the profit made on each gram, and how many days the company's cash stays locked in metal.

What changed in Q2 FY27

Revenue fell 27% to Rs 15,836 cr, yet group profit hit a record Rs 120 cr. The core company's profit rose 2.5 times to Rs 85 cr. The reason: Augmont bought much more old gold (scrap), which pays a better margin but ties up cash for longer. Scrap rose from about 3% to 24.5% of all buying. A one-off silver import advantage also helped.

What to watch

Cash. In the first half of FY27 the business used Rs 578 cr of cash in operations while reporting Rs 181 cr of profit. Money owed by customers jumped to Rs 749 cr. Management says this was month-end sales that were collected in early October. One promoter-group company bought 27% of FY26 sales. Management gives no numerical guidance.

Industry

India is one of the world's biggest gold buyers, and most of the trade is still informal

Indians hold about 31,000 tonnes of gold at home, worth roughly US$3.4 trillion. That is close to the value of the whole Indian stock market (US$5.1 trillion), according to figures the company cites from the World Gold Council. Yet most gold is still bought and sold through small, informal dealers.

Gold reaches buyers in steps: it is imported or recycled, refined to a standard purity, sold to jewellers in bars, and finally sold to people as jewellery or coins. Each step earns a small margin. Augmont works in four of the five steps.

In short

Household gold is almost as large as the Indian stock market.

Annual demand is about 700-800 tonnes. 2026 is expected at 650-750 t as high prices cut jewellery buying.

Investment buying (coins, bars, ETFs) is rising while jewellery is falling.

Recycling old gold is a big, growing supply source. This matters for Augmont (see page 8).

Industry and competition

Augmont is one of the largest refiners, and new gold products are growing fast

Refining is concentrated in a few players. Augmont's two plants, at Rudrapur (Uttarakhand) and Navi Mumbai, give it 284 tonnes a year of capacity. On the company's own comparison that is second only to MMTC-PAMP. Capacity is not the same as output, though. Augmont's plants ran far below capacity in FY26 (page 10).

Business model

What Augmont does: two platforms running on one engine

Think of Augmont as a wholesale gold shop with a factory behind it. It buys metal from banks, importers and people selling old gold. It refines some of it. Then it sells bars to jewellers (the big business) and coins or digital gold to the public (the small, fast-growing business).

In short

SPOT (business buyers) is 93% of revenue. Gold For All (consumers) is 7% and growing fastest.

Both use the same refineries, pricing engine and delivery network.

Augmont does not lend money in gold loans. Partner lender Finkurve does; Augmont supplies the technology.

Jewellery for EMI sales is made by outside partners.

How it earns

Augmont earns a small spread on a very large volume, so speed of cash matters

Augmont is a principal, not a broker. It buys the metal, owns it for a short time, and sells it. It charges no brokerage. It earns the gap between its buying and selling price (the spread), plus refining and handling fees on old gold.

Because the full metal value is booked as revenue, sales look enormous and margins look tiny. A better way to judge the business is: how much profit does each rupee of its own capital earn in a year? That depends on two things: the spread per trade, and how many times the same cash can be reused.

A simple example (illustration, not company data). If Rs 100 of capital is reused every 2 days, it can do about 150 trades a year. If it is reused every 4 days, only about 75. So old gold must earn at least twice the spread per trade just to match the yearly profit from imported gold. Management says it does earn more, but has not disclosed the exact spreads.

Banks generally will not lend against gold bought for trading, management says. So growth needs the company's own money (equity). That is why most of the IPO money went into working capital (page 16).

In short

Profit per Rs 100 of sales went from 9 paise (FY21) to 76 paise (Q2 FY27).

Revenue mostly reflects gold prices and volumes, not pricing power.

The key numbers to track: spread per gram and days of cash tied up. The company does not disclose either directly.

Enterprise platform (SPOT)

Enterprise revenue rose, but tonnes sold fell, because gold got dearer

The enterprise business sells bars to jewellers, dealers, manufacturers, fund houses and overseas buyers. SPOT, the online counter, is most of it. Buyers see a live price, place an order, and receive the metal within about two working days from one of 20 delivery centres.

When gold prices rise, revenue can grow even as fewer tonnes are sold. That is exactly what happened in FY26 and again in Q2 FY27. Management's explanation: the business has a fixed pool of cash, and at higher prices the same cash buys fewer tonnes.

Sourcing

Old gold (scrap) jumped from 3% to 24.5% of buying, lifting margins

Augmont buys gold from five places: Indian banks that import it, foreign banks, dore (semi-refined) imports, the IIBX exchange in GIFT City, and old gold from jewellers and the public. Old gold has to be tested and refined before it can be sold as bars.

In the last year Augmont pushed hard into old gold. It now makes up about a quarter of all buying. Management gives three reasons: it earns a better margin (spread plus a refining fee), it builds loyalty with jewellers, and it supports the government's aim of cutting imports. The cost is that cash stays tied up for 3-4 days instead of about 2.

Two separate profit boosts in Q2 FY27. 1) More old gold: management calls this lasting ("structural"). 2) Silver: when silver imports were restricted, Augmont held a government (DGFT) licence and imported via IIBX while others struggled. Management itself calls this "situational", meaning it should not be expected to repeat. The split between the two was not disclosed.

In short

Buying more old gold raised profit per gram but slowed cash turns.

Part of the Q2 profit came from a temporary silver import advantage.

Management expects scrap to stay at 'healthy levels' but gave no number.

Watch: scrap share and margin in Q3 FY27.

Consumer platform (Gold For All)

The consumer business is small but growing fastest

Gold For All sells gold to ordinary people in small amounts. It works through apps and partners rather than its own shops: 245 partner brands embed Augmont's digital gold, 751 channel partners sell coins, and products are also sold on Zepto, Amazon and Flipkart.

It is only 7% of revenue, but it is growing much faster than the enterprise side. In Q2 FY27 consumer revenue rose 47% to Rs 1,984 cr, about 13% of the quarter's revenue.

A note on user numbers. Augmont reports 52.3 million registered consumers (Sep 2026). That counts sign-ups across partner apps, not active buyers. The company does not disclose active users or repeat rates.

In short

Consumer revenue grew from Rs 1,178 cr (FY24) to Rs 6,687 cr (FY26).

Coins and digital gold drive most of it.

Average purchase size more than doubled to Rs 548, helped by higher gold prices.

Registered users (52.3 mn) are not the same as active customers.

Group structure

More than half of group profit comes from subsidiaries

Augmont is a group of six companies. The listed parent runs SPOT and the refineries. Two subsidiaries, Ideal Fiscal Services and Augmont Goldtech, earned more than half of FY26 group profit. Small outside shareholders own 9% and 3.6% of them, so a slice of their profit does not belong to Augmont's shareholders.

This explains why the company talks about two different profit numbers. The parent ("core") profit rose 2.5 times in Q2 FY27. Group profit belonging to shareholders rose only 11%, from Rs 106.5 cr to Rs 118.4 cr, because the rest of the group (subsidiaries, minority share and consolidation adjustments) contributed Rs 39 cr less than a year earlier. The filings do not break this down. Investors should use the group figure.

In short

Parent: 44% of FY26 group profit. Ideal Fiscal: 39%. Goldtech: 18%.

The 'core profit up 2.5x' headline is the parent only.

Profit belonging to Augmont shareholders rose 11% in Q2 FY27.

Minority holders' share of FY26 profit: Rs 14.4 cr.

Track record

Six years of fast growth, with high returns on a small equity base

Over five years revenue grew 4.6x and profit about 18x. Most of the jump came in FY25 and FY26, helped by rising gold prices, a bigger consumer business and better margins. Return on equity has been very high (about 50-74%) because the business needs little equipment and profit was earned on a small equity base.

Return ratios will look lower for a while. Equity nearly doubled after the IPO in August 2026, and that cash needs time to earn. Annualised H1 FY27 ROE was 27%.

In short

Revenue 4.6x and profit about 18x from FY21 to FY26.

ROE of 51% in FY26 is high, but will fall as IPO money is put to work.

Growth was helped by gold prices; tonnes sold did not grow the same way (page 7).

Cash flow

Profit is not yet turning into cash

This is the most important open question. A trading business should turn profit into cash quickly. Augmont has not done so recently. From FY22 to FY26 it reported Rs 723 cr of profit but only Rs 158 cr of cash from operations. In H1 FY27 operations used Rs 578 cr while profit was Rs 181 cr. The IPO money largely filled this gap.

Management's explanation: large sales on the last days of September were collected in the first days of October, so the 30 Sep snapshot looks worse than normal. That may be right, and the Q3 results will show it. But in FY26 too, most receivables were owed by a single related company (page 17), and operating cash was negative. Two clean quarters of cash would go a long way.

In short

FY22-FY26: Rs 723 cr profit, Rs 158 cr operating cash.

H1 FY27: Rs 578 cr cash used vs Rs 181 cr profit.

Receivables went from Rs 182 cr (Mar 2026) to Rs 749 cr (Sep 2026).

Watch the Q3 FY27 balance sheet: did receivables come back down?

IPO and use of money

IPO money went straight into working capital

Augmont listed in August 2026 at Rs 788 a share. The issue had two parts. In the offer for sale, three promoter-family members sold shares for Rs 205 cr; that money went to them, not to the company. In the fresh issue, the company raised Rs 620 cr of new money.

Almost all of it was spent on working capital, meaning metal and supplier advances, within weeks. That matches the business need explained on page 6. It also means future growth needs more profit kept in the business, or more equity. Management would not say how much extra revenue or profit the new money should bring.

The selling shareholders' average cost was Rs 1.86 a share (prospectus p.1). Promoters still own 81.9%. No promoter shares were pledged at the IPO.

In short

Rs 620 cr fresh issue; Rs 573 cr after costs.

Rs 465 cr earmarked for working capital, Rs 108 cr for general use.

The company says all of it was used by 30 Sep 2026.

A CARE Ratings monitoring agency reports on use each quarter.

Which yardstick fits this business?

Price to sales is not useful: revenue mostly reflects the gold price, and Augmont keeps under Rs 1 of every Rs 100 of sales. EV/EBITDA adds little: the company has almost no debt and tiny depreciation, so EBITDA and profit move together. Price to earnings (P/E) is our main yardstick, on profit that belongs to Augmont's shareholders (after the minority partners' share), and on the full 9.14 cr shares in issue after the IPO. Price to book is the cross-check: profit is capped by how much capital the company has, because banks do not fund gold stock for trading. So a P/E only makes sense if the return on equity behind it does too.

About 5.0x book value after the IPO.

Key assumptions and caveats

  • Guided vs estimated. Nothing here is company guidance. Management declined to give FY27 numbers (call p.14). Sales, margins, other income, interest, depreciation, tax (25.2%) and minority share (3.5% of profit) are all our estimates. Interest, depreciation and tax are the least certain bridge lines. The Q2 FY27 finance cost of Rs 18 cr is not explained in the filings, so we keep a similar cost in every case.
  • Capital check. Banks do not fund gold stock for trading (call p.12), so growth must come from retained profit. We assume no dividend and no new shares. In every case, sales per rupee of equity stays at about 36-43x, close to the Q2 FY27 level of about 37x (annualised). Faster growth than this would need more equity, which would dilute EPS.
  • Shares. 9.14 cr shares after the IPO (prospectus p.107). No further dilution is disclosed. ESOPs or options, if any, are not captured.
  • Multiples. P/E is held flat across the years within each case and differs between cases (bear 14x, base 20x, bull 26x vs about 25x today). That is a simplification. In reality the multiple moves with sentiment and with proof of cash conversion.
  • Gold price. We assume no big change in the rupee gold price. Higher prices raise sales but cut the tonnes the same capital can buy, so profit moves much less than sales.
  • One-offs. No repeat of the Q2 FY27 silver import gain is assumed. Other income includes derivative gains that can swing either way.
  • Not modelled. A sum-of-the-parts view (SPOT, Gold For All, refining, EGR) is possible but segment profit is not disclosed. EGR income is not included in any case.
  • These are scenario bands, not forecasts. This is not investment advice, and the author is not acting as a licensed financial adviser.
