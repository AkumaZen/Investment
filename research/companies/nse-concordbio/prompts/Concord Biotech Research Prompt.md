## 0A. Why we are covering this business

Written from the documents in plain words, in under 15 lines.

- What it is: Concord Biotech grows microbes in 1,250 m3 of steel fermentation tanks in Gujarat to make complex drug ingredients (APIs), mainly for organ-transplant drugs, and sells them by the kilogram to generic drug makers in 70+ countries [deck p18, p14]. Since 2016 it also turns its own APIs into tablets, capsules and now injectables [deck p17].
- Why it caught our attention: after years of steady growth, FY26 revenue fell 12% to Rs 1,055 cr and EBITDA margin fell from 42% to 35% [Screener], yet H1 FY27 revenue is back up 25.6% to Rs 566.6 cr [deck p10].
- Why now: Q2 FY27 results (27 Oct 2026) showed API sales up 30% [deck p7]; management repeated 18-19%+ growth and a 38-40% margin path on the 28 Oct 2026 call [call p8, p12]; a 1:1 bonus was allotted on 4 Nov 2026 [BSE ann].
- The core debate: was FY26 a one-year pause by cautious customers that is now reversing, with margins back to 38-40% as Unit III (55% used), Unit II (21%) and Unit IV (5%) fill up [call p18], or has the business stepped down to slower growth and lower margins?
- What the market seems to believe: at Rs 594 the market value is Rs 12,418 cr, 44.5x trailing PAT of Rs 279 cr [Screener]; at 30x in FY29 the price needs PAT of about Rs 414 cr, a 14% yearly rise for three years [E], so the market already assumes a recovery.
- What would make us care, and what would make us drop it: we care if H2 FY27 revenue reaches about Rs 689 cr (the level 19% full-year growth needs, +14% on H2 FY26) with EBITDA margin above 36%; we drop it if Q3 FY27 API growth falls below 10% or Unit IV has no signed injectable contract by the Q4 FY27 call.
- What the reader will be able to decide: whether the recovery is real and priced, what value range the guided path supports against Rs 594, and which dated numbers to watch to know early if it is going wrong.

## 0B. How to think

Concord is a fermentation API maker with a newer, loss-making formulation and injectable arm attached.
Its value depends on two things: whether the core API business is back to its old growth after the FY26 fall, and whether the new plants built in FY21 to FY25 earn a return.
The job is NOT to retell the investor presentation; the job is to prove, with numbers from 14 calls, 14 decks and 3 annual reports, whether FY26 was a pause or a step down, and what the new capacity is really worth.

### Connecting the dots

Fixed assets rose from Rs 575 cr in FY24 to Rs 795 cr in FY25 as CWIP fell from Rs 211 cr to Rs 51 cr [Screener]: Unit IV (injectables) and the formulation expansion went into use.
Depreciation then rose from Rs 54 cr in FY25 to Rs 74 cr in FY26 [Screener], while Unit IV still runs at 5% utilisation [call p18].
Management says EBITDA margin excluding the injectables unit and Stellon would be 37.1% in Q2 FY27, against 33.8% reported [call p7, deck p6]: 3.3 points on Rs 309.1 cr is about Rs 10.2 cr of EBITDA in one quarter, and 4.8 points on H1 sales of Rs 566.6 cr is about Rs 27.2 cr in the half [E].
So roughly Rs 40-55 cr a year of EBITDA cost plus about Rs 20 cr of extra depreciation sits in units that earn almost nothing yet [E]; that is about a fifth of TTM PAT of Rs 279 cr before tax effects.
For the thesis this means the margin gap is mostly a fill-the-plant question, and the evidence to track is signed injectable contracts and Unit IV utilisation, not the API business.

## 6A. Visual intelligence

The goal is not more charts. Think like an equity research analyst and a data-visualization designer: understand the information, find the main insight, choose the right form and show it clearly.

Preservation rule: this section adds to sections 0 to 9 and S1 to S20 and replaces none of them. It removes no business or financial analysis. It does not change the research method, the design system, the section 8 checks, the model assignments or the document workflow. External skills are never required, and presentation never reduces the depth of business understanding. The report must stay readable, responsive and printable.

Five questions before any visual:
1. What information are we trying to communicate?
2. What is the most important insight in this data?
3. What comparison helps the reader understand it?
4. Which form shows this most accurately?
5. What context or caveat must go with it?

Choosing the form:
- Table: when exact figures and detailed comparisons matter most.
- Chart: when patterns, relationships, trends or composition are easier to see.
- Diagram: when explaining how the business operates, how money flows or how parts connect.
- Nothing: no decorative charts, and no forcing data into a chart.
- One y-axis per chart. Two measures on different scales become two charts, or are indexed to a common base.
- A single headline number is inline emphasis or a compact stat, not a one-bar chart. Part-to-whole is ranked bars or a segmented bar, not a pie.
- Available forms: comparison tables, time-series lines, grouped and stacked bars, waterfalls, contribution charts, capacity timelines, Sankey diagrams, value-chain diagrams, geographic and process diagrams, financial bridges, segment matrices, risk matrices, and actual vs guidance comparisons.

Data storytelling rules:
- Titles state the insight, such as "Revenue grew 42%, but EBITDA margin fell as new capacity ramped up", and only when the evidence supports it.
- The most important number or change is highlighted; context is greyed.
- Historical, current and projected figures are visibly distinct. Projected figures are hatched or lighter and labelled [E].
- Reported figures, estimates [E] and management guidance [G] are kept separate.
- Units, periods and the comparison base are shown on every visual.
- Unusual movements and divergences are explained next to the visual.
- The source sits directly under the visual.
- Axes and scales are never manipulated, and missing data is never created. A gap stays a gap, labelled [Not Disclosed].

Presentation priorities:
1. Clear information hierarchy.
2. Consistent typography and spacing.
3. Insight-led section introductions.
4. Financial tables that are easy to compare.
5. Charts that explain business economics.
6. Management commentary placed beside the numbers it explains.
7. Key findings visibly distinct from supporting information.
8. Traceable sources and assumptions.
9. No excessive cards, icons, borders or decoration.
10. No repeating the same data in several visual forms.

Library rule: Chart.js and inline SVG only, as set in section 7. Waterfalls, Sankeys, bridges, value chains and timelines are drawn in inline SVG or Chart.js, with no new library. Plotly, D3 or Python charts are used only if the user asks or the output format changes.
Cards rule: use plain tables, narrative and hairline rules under the reference-driven section 7. A single number can be an inline emphasis, not a mandatory card. The historical thick-border card style is superseded by the 7 November 2026 reference-design amendment.
